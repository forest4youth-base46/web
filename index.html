Reference

Clinical reference

Contraindications and screening for forest-based and immersive virtual nature work. Evidence-informed starting points — not protocols. Clinical judgement governs all applications.

Likely to make forest work unsuitable for now

Case by case

Active psychosis

Acute suicidality

Severe anxiety disorders preventing outdoor participation

Eating disorders with active physical risk, unless movement can be realistically restricted

Any condition where physical exertion is clinically contra-indicated

Symptom severity, not diagnosis, is the primary criterion. The clinical team screens before the first session, keeps suitability under review, and decides case by case, knowing the young person. Where forest work isn't right yet, nature connection can be introduced gradually, in a sensory garden or through immersive virtual nature, with the forest held as a later goal.

Before the first session: the assessment
Contraindication

Mobility and physical access

Can the young person physically access the planned site? Are there terrain, weather or exertion factors to account for?

Contraindication

Sensory sensitivities

Will the outdoor environment be therapeutic or overwhelming? Consider noise, texture, temperature, unpredictability.

Contraindication

Safety

What is the risk of self-harm, elopement or substance use in an uncontrolled environment?

Contraindication

Readiness

Is the young person open to a nature-based approach? Have they been given clear information about what it involves?

Contraindication

Relational readiness

Is there an established therapeutic relationship with at least one adult in the group?

Contraindication

Physical health

Any physical health conditions, including medication side effects, that could be affected by outdoor activity, weather or exertion?

Immersive virtual nature: screen before first use
Contraindication

Photosensitive epilepsy

Check for a personal or family history of seizures or photosensitivity. Avoid flickering, flashing or rapidly changing high-contrast content. Calm nature scenes are usually low-risk, but verify first.

Contraindication

Motion sensitivity and cybersickness

Ask about motion sickness and migraine. Cybersickness can occur with headsets and immersive rooms alike, and may appear during the session or in the hours after, so monitor tolerance during the first exposure and check again later.

Contraindication

Clinical and diagnostic profile

Some presentations, for example active psychosis, marked dissociation or certain trauma histories, call for more caution, more support, or may mean immersive virtual nature is not appropriate at this time.

Contraindication

Trauma-linked content

Anticipate environments or elements that could reactivate difficult material, and adapt the content before the session.

Contraindication

Headset age rating

For headset use, check the manufacturer's age rating (many are 12–13+). The immersive room avoids this hardware limit.

Session Builder

Session Tools & Activity Guides

Practical resources to plan, run, and reflect on forest-based sessions.

How to use this section

Activities are grouped under five themes that map onto the therapeutic arc: Getting There; Waking Up Your Senses; Discovering Yourself; Connecting with Others; and Coming Back to Yourself. Within each group, activities range from lower to higher demand. Select based on where the group is on a given day, not on where the session plan says they should be.

The principle of invitation rather than task applies throughout. No activity is mandatory, and a young person who watches rather than participates is participating.

Session details

Optional — included in the export if filled in.

The table below sets out general practice guidance that will support key preferences such as safety, sensory preferences, regulation, trust, and autonomy. It is indicative; care teams should add to it based on the young person they know.

Before each session. Tap each item to mark it.

This checklist does not replace a full risk assessment for clinical settings.

Before

Before a young person reaches the forest, the therapeutic relationship has to be in place. Structural and logistical arrangements for site, roles and risk must be sorted. The young person must be prepared for an environment that is unfamiliar and unpredictable.

Threshold

The shift from resistance or uncertainty to acceptance. This may not happen in the first session or at a predictable point. It is less a moment in time and more a metaphorical crossing, when the forest is given permission to do its work.

During

The active time in the forest, where the planned structure creates the conditions for unplanned, spontaneous experience. The forest acts as a partner: it makes offers, initiates encounters, and shapes what happens in ways neither clinician nor young person anticipated.

After

Transition back to everyday life. Consistent closing rituals mark a boundary between the forest world and the clinical world, and are part of what makes the experience therapeutic.

Reflect & Evaluate

Post-Session Reflection

Prompts and indicators to support practitioner reflection after each session.

Session just run
1

What did you notice in yourself?

Were there moments you felt drawn to redirect, speed up, or fill silence? What does that tell you about your own relationship to the material or the environment?

2

What did the environment do?

Did anything in the natural space shift the session unexpectedly — weather, an animal, a sound? How did you and the participant respond? Was that response worked with or avoided?

3

What would you do differently?

Was the activity well-matched to the participant's state? Was the timing right? Where did the frame hold and where did it need flexibility?

4

What needs to be brought to supervision?

Note any countertransference, boundary moments, clinical concerns, or powerful projective material that emerged. FI supervision should include the environmental dimension explicitly.
